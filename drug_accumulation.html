Drug Accumulation

Theory

Constant-rate infusion is uncommon for chronic therapy. Almost everything taken long-term — metformin 500 mg twice daily, an antibiotic every 8 hours — is a series of discrete doses at a fixed interval. The concentration still rises toward a plateau, like an infusion, but it does so as a sawtooth rather than a smooth curve.

Why Accumulation Happens

Accumulation occurs because there is always some drug left in the body from previous doses when the next one arrives. Once the plateau is reached, there's no further increase from one interval to the next — the amount lost to elimination during an interval exactly equals the dose given at the start of that interval.

The plateau is reached after 5 half-lives, exactly like infusion steady state — and just like infusion, this is independent of the dosing interval. What the dosing interval determines is something else entirely: how high that plateau sits, and how much the concentration swings within each interval.

Peak-to-Trough Fluctuation

The ratio of Cmax,SS to Cmin,SS describes how much the concentration swings within a dosing interval. A drug with a wide therapeutic window (e.g. 20–100 mg/L, a 5-fold range) can tolerate large swings; a drug with a narrow window (e.g. 40–80 mg/L, only 2-fold) cannot — the same RA that's perfectly fine for one drug could push a narrow-window drug outside its safe range at the peak, or below effective levels at the trough.

Higher accumulation and smaller fluctuation come together (short τ relative to t½); lower accumulation and larger fluctuation also come together (long τ relative to t½). You cannot independently maximize accumulation and minimize fluctuation — the dosing interval trades one against the other.
