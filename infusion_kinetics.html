IV Infusion Kinetics

Theory

Continuous IV infusion delivers drug at a constant, zero-order rate (k0) rather than as a single dose. The concentration rises as long as the input rate exceeds elimination, and levels off once the two are equal — steady state.

At steady state, rate in equals rate out (dX/dt = 0), which gives:

Css depends only on k0 and Cl — not on Vd. Doubling Cl halves Css; Vd never enters the equation. What Vd does affect is how long it takes to get there:

Time to steady state is conventionally taken as 5 half-lives (~97% of Css) — governed entirely by K, and therefore by both Cl and Vd. A larger Vd or a smaller Cl both mean a longer half-life and a longer wait to plateau, even though only Cl changes where that plateau sits.

Stopping the Infusion

Once an infusion stops at time T, the input term disappears and the concentration simply declines like an IV bolus from whatever concentration was present at that moment — regardless of whether steady state had been reached:

Reaching Steady State Faster

For a drug with a long half-life, waiting 5 half-lives on continuous infusion alone may be impractical (an 8-hour half-life needs ~40 hours). Two strategies front-load the dose so the plateau is reached immediately or very quickly:

Bolus Loading Dose + Infusion

A bolus equal to the target amount in the body is given at t=0, alongside starting the maintenance infusion. The bolus decays while the infusion rises — the two are complementary, and the sum sits at Css throughout.

Loading Infusion + Maintenance

For narrow-therapeutic-window drugs where a bolus spike is too risky, a brief infusion at the maximum allowable rate (k0*) reaches Css over a short, deliberate duration T, then switches to the maintenance rate.

Both strategies use the same maintenance rate once Css is established: k0 = Css×Cl.
